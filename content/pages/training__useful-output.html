Getting output you can actually use

Most people's experience of AI writing is a draft that's fluent, generic and needs more editing than writing it would have. That's a method problem, and it's fixable in an hour.

The problem with the first draft

Ask for a client email and you get a client email: grammatical, structured, and written for nobody in particular. It reads like a template because functionally it is one — the tool had nothing specific to work with, so it produced the average of everything it has seen.

The fix isn't a clever prompt. It's giving the tool the same four things you'd give a capable new employee before asking them to draft something.

The four things

  1. Context. Who is reading this, what do they already know, what happened before, and what should happen after they read it.
  2. Examples. Two or three of your own past documents of the same type. This single step does more for voice than any instruction about tone.
  3. Constraints. Length, structure, what must be included, what must not be mentioned, reading level, and any phrase your industry can't use.
  4. A rejection. The first output is a starting position. Saying what's wrong with it specifically is faster than rewriting it, and it's the habit most people never form.

The test we use in the room

Take a document you've written forty times. Get a draft you would send without editing. If you can't, the gap tells you which of the four things is missing — and it's almost always examples.

What this looks like by the end

  • A first draft you correct rather than rewrite, on the documents you produce most often.
  • A written brief for your three most common documents, saved where the team can use it.
  • A clear sense of which jobs are worth the setup time and which are faster by hand.

Where it goes next

Once the output is usable, the next question is how to stop rebuilding the setup every time — that's building reusable setups. If the documents you care about are long, working with long documents comes first.
