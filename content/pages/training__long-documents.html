Working with long documents

This is where these tools are genuinely strong and where the failure mode is most expensive: a fluent summary of a document it skimmed.

The work this covers

  • A contract or bundle where the question is narrow and the reading isn't.
  • A ruling, standard or Practice Standards revision where you only need what changed.
  • A strata report, tribunal document or scope of works you have to price against.
  • Your own notes, minutes or transcripts turned into a document with a structure.

Ask a question, don't ask for a summary

“Summarise this” is the weakest possible instruction: it has no criteria, so you get the average of what summaries look like. “What obligations fall on the tenant, and where does each one appear” has criteria, and it can be checked.

Everything in this module comes back to that: named questions, in your own words, with a requirement to point at where the answer came from so you can verify it in seconds.

The habits that stop the expensive mistake

  1. Require a location for every claim — clause, page, heading. Unverifiable answers get thrown out.
  2. Ask the same question twice in different words. Inconsistent answers mean it hasn't actually found the thing.
  3. Ask what's missing or unclear, not only what's there. This is where these tools earn their keep.
  4. Spot-check two answers against the document by hand. Every time, not just the first week.

Long is not unlimited

Very large documents still get truncated or handled in pieces depending on tool and tier, and a tool that has quietly dropped half the input will still answer confidently. Knowing how to detect that is part of the module.

Why Claude usually, and when not

Long input with one careful answer is the shape Claude handles best, and it is the default we teach for this module — see Claude for long documents. Short documents at high volume are the opposite shape and go the other way.
