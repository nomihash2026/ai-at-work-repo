Writing your team's AI policy

One page, in your words, that answers which tools, which tier, which data and who to ask. Long policies get filed. Short ones get followed.

What it has to answer

  1. Which tools are approved, and on which account or tier.
  2. What never goes in, listed specifically for this business.
  3. What must be checked before anything leaves the building, and by whom.
  4. What has to be disclosed — to clients, funders, insurers or a court.
  5. Who to ask when the answer isn't obvious, by name.

Why most policies fail

Two reasons. They're written for office staff on laptops, so they mean nothing to someone on a shift with a phone. And they ban rather than direct, which produces exactly the unreported use the policy was meant to prevent.

A policy that says “no AI tools” in a business where six people are already using them has made the problem invisible rather than smaller. A rule that says which account to use, and for what, gets followed and can be improved.

Making it hold across a casual workforce

  • Written at the reading level of the newest casual, not the operations manager.
  • Attached to induction, not to a shared drive nobody opens.
  • Framed around the tasks people actually do on shift.
  • Reviewed when the tools change, with a visible date on it.

Insurers and funders will ask

Professional indemnity insurers, NDIS auditors and some funders now ask whether a written AI policy exists. Having one that matches what staff actually do is a different answer from having one that doesn't.
