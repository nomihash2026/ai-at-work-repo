Where the line sits

Participant detail stays out until the tool, the tier and the written rule are all settled — and the rule has to hold across a large casual workforce, not just the office.

The boundary we teach

No clinical content, no decisions about a participant's supports or funding, no participant identifying detail.

Audit exposure is the lever. Documentation is simultaneously the biggest time sink and the first thing an auditor reads, which is why documentation and policy come before general team training. The hard part is a rule that holds across a shift-based casual workforce.

The family-level rule for health & care: Nothing identifiable about a patient, client or participant goes into a general-purpose AI tool. Not the name, not the record, not the detail that would identify them to someone who knows them.

The questions we get asked

Our support workers won't follow a policy.

Most AI policies fail because they were written for office staff. The rule taught here has to work for someone on a shift with a phone, which changes how it is written.

We're mid-audit cycle.

That's usually the best time. Documentation quality and evidence assembly is the work, and the audit gives it a deadline.

Isn't participant data completely off limits?

Yes. The productivity comes from structure, consistency and de-identified drafting, not from feeding records into a tool.

Regulatory context

Relevant bodies and frameworks: NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission, NDIS Practice Standards, Privacy Act 1988. NDIS Practice Standards are revised periodically. The current version is confirmed before any engagement and no version number appears on the page without a check.

We name no CPD hour figures anywhere on this site. Requirements differ by body and by state, and an out-of-date number is worse than none.
