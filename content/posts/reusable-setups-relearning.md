Here's the pattern that separates the businesses still getting value from AI a year later from the ones that tried it and drifted.

It isn't skill, and it isn't which tool they chose. It's whether anything got saved.

A team that saved nothing starts every task from an empty box. Which means every task is a small act of remembering: what did I ask for last time, what format do we use, what did the good version look like. At 4pm on a busy Thursday, that effort loses to the old way, every time. So the tool gets used for the easy things and abandoned for the work that actually mattered.

## What a setup is, and what it isn't

A saved prompt is one instruction you paste again. Useful, minor.

A setup is the surrounding context, stored once: your house style, two or three good past outputs as examples, the structure your documents follow, the things you never say, and who checks the result. With that in place, the request itself collapses to three or four facts — the ones only you hold.

That's the actual mechanism. Not better phrasing, less remembering.

Both tools have a version of this. Claude has [Projects](/tools/claude/projects/); ChatGPT has [saved instructions and custom setups](/tools/chatgpt/custom-setups/). They work differently and the principle is identical.

## What goes in, and what absolutely doesn't

**In — the things true every time:**

- The format, including how long you actually want the output to be.
- Two or three real past outputs you were happy with. Showing beats describing, and this single item does more than any amount of instruction-writing.
- Your vocabulary: what you call things, and what you never call them.
- The structure, section by section, if your documents follow one.
- The checking step, written into the setup so it travels with it.

**Out:**

- Anything from your never list. A setup is shared and durable, which makes it exactly the wrong place for client, patient or participant information. This is the most common place a careful team slips, because the material goes in once, months ago, and nobody thinks of the setup as a document.
- Anything true only this week. That belongs in the request.

:::checklist A setup is finished when | Someone who didn't build it can run it and get your format | The request is a handful of facts, not a paragraph of instruction | The checking step is named, with a person against it | There's a note saying what to change when the template changes | Nothing inside it would be a problem if it were shared with the whole team

## The failure that kills setups quietly

Not a technical one. Three people end up with three versions of the same setup on three individual accounts, each edited slightly, and nobody notices until a client receives two documents that don't match.

The fix is boring: one owner per document type, and the setup's content written down somewhere outside the tool — a page in your own systems, which also means it survives someone leaving. That page is the step teams skip, and skipping it is how a setup ends up stranded with whoever happened to be in the room when it was built.

:::ask If your team has quietly grown three versions of the same document, that's a good first job to fix, and it's usually a short piece of work.

## Why this block decides whether training was worth it

A session that teaches method produces a good afternoon. A session that ends with two or three setups built on your real documents produces a change that's still there next month, because the cost of getting a good result on one named job has dropped to nearly zero.

That's why [reusable setups](/training/reusable-setups/) sits last in the curriculum and why a session that runs out of time before reaching it has failed, regardless of how well the first three hours went. It's also the single clearest of the [five reasons training doesn't stick](/insights/training-that-didnt-stick/).

## Start with one

Not five. Take the job you've already chosen — and if you haven't, [here's how to choose one](/insights/pick-the-one-job/) — and build one setup properly, including the written page. Run it on the next live instance of the job, not on a practice run, because the practice run is always kinder.

Then leave it for a few weeks and see what breaks. Whatever breaks it will break the next five the same way, and knowing that before you build them is worth more than the four setups you didn't build.
