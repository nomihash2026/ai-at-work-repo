Walk into a real estate agency and you'll find two businesses sharing a logo, a database and a kitchen.

Sales runs on pitching, listing, negotiating and following up. Property management runs on compliance, coordination, and answering the same question from four different people. Both are writing-heavy. Almost nothing they write is the same document.

Which is why training them in one room produces the outcome you'd expect: half the people are engaged for half the time, and the examples on the table always belong to somebody else.

## What sales actually needs

The starting condition is unusual: **agents are frequently already using these tools personally.** Not sanctioned, not consistently, and often on the free tier, but the habit exists. So the useful work isn't introduction — it's improvement, plus a boundary nobody has drawn.

The jobs worth building on:

- Listing copy that sounds like the property rather than like every other listing, produced in minutes rather than at 9pm after a day of appointments.
- The follow-up sequence after an open home, including the second and third contacts nobody has time to send.
- Appraisal and pitch documents, which are templated and where the specifics are what matter.
- Vendor updates that explain a quiet fortnight without sounding defensive.
- Turning a voice note recorded between appointments into a usable draft — one of the highest-value habits in a job done mostly from a car.

The risk in sales is different too. It isn't usually privacy; it's a confident claim about a property that turns out to be wrong. Which means the checking step is about factual accuracy against the actual property, and it needs to be written down rather than assumed.

## What property management actually needs

A heavier and more repetitive writing load, with more consequence attached.

- Routine inspection reports and the letters that follow them.
- Arrears, breach and entry notices, where the wording is partly prescribed and getting it wrong has consequences.
- Coordinating tradespeople, owners and tenants on the same job — three audiences, three tones, one set of facts.
- Owner updates explaining a decision that the owner will not like.
- The same tenancy question answered for the fourth time this week.

:::checklist Where property management workload actually concentrates | Anything that has to say the same thing to three different audiences | Documents where part of the wording is prescribed and part is yours | Jobs that arrive in volume with a deadline attached | Correspondence written in a hurry that gets read carefully later

The boundary matters more on this side, because property management handles tenancy records, identity documents and financial circumstances as a matter of routine. A general "be careful" is not a rule. Naming the document types is — which is exactly what the [never list](/guides/what-never-goes-in/) is for, rewritten in your own terms.

## The structural reason to split the sessions

It isn't just the task lists. The two halves differ in ways that change the training design:

**Volume versus length.** Property management runs on high-volume short correspondence; sales runs on fewer, longer, more persuasive documents. Those pull toward different tools, which is the whole point of [the reach-for-this rule](/insights/which-tool-rule-of-thumb/).

**Who checks.** In sales, the agent is usually the last set of eyes. In property management, there's often a principal or licensee accountable for documents someone else drafted, so the checking step has to be explicit and owned.

**Existing habits.** Sales arrives with habits to correct. Property management often arrives with none, and a room where half the people are unlearning while the other half is starting cold moves at the wrong speed for everyone.

:::ask If one side of the agency is further behind than the other, that's usually where the first session goes. Happy to work out which on a call.

## What this looks like in practice

Two focused sessions, usually a couple of weeks apart, sharing the same foundations and nothing else. Same six modules underneath — [useful output](/training/useful-output/), the tool rule, long documents, reusable setups, the never list, the written policy — with completely different examples on the table.

The shared piece that should happen once, for everyone including the principal: the boundary and the written rule. That's the one conversation where having both halves of the agency in the room is an advantage, because the rule has to be the same rule.
