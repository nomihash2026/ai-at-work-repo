Every few months a new model arrives, someone publishes a benchmark table, and a business that standardised on one tool last year wonders whether it chose wrong.

That's an expensive way to think about it. The useful question isn't which tool is better. It's which shape of job you're holding — because the shapes are stable even when the models aren't.

## Three shapes, not two

Most advice frames this as a two-way choice. It's three, and the third one is the one that keeps you out of trouble.

**Long, consequential, voice-sensitive work.** A forty-page contract you need summarised against a checklist. A tender response that has to sound like your firm. A policy document rewritten for a different audience without losing its meaning. Work where the input is long, the reasoning runs over several steps, and a shift in tone halfway through is a real defect. This is where we point people at Claude, and where [long documents and bundles](/tools/claude/long-documents/) is the module that earns its place.

**High-volume, fast-turnaround, spoken-in work.** Twenty follow-ups that each need to be slightly different. A voice note from the van turned into a quote email before the next job. A photo of a handwritten site note that needs to become a paragraph. Work where speed and input flexibility beat sustained polish. That's the ChatGPT side, and [voice note to finished draft](/tools/chatgpt/voice-to-draft/) is the one that changes trade and field businesses most.

**Work that goes to neither.** Clinical judgement. Financial advice. A tax position, as distinct from tax admin. A safety sign-off. Anything where a wrong output is a regulated harm rather than an inconvenience, and anything needing expertise nobody in the room actually holds.

That third category is not a disclaimer. It's the reason the first two are safe to teach quickly.

## Why the split holds up

Because it isn't about features. It's about what each kind of job punishes.

Long-document work punishes drift — a summary that's accurate for twelve pages and subtly wrong for the last three is worse than no summary. Sustained, consistent handling over a long input is the thing being bought.

High-volume work punishes friction. If getting the draft takes three exchanges, the person goes back to typing it themselves, and you've bought nothing. Speed of entry, including talking to it, is the thing being bought.

Those two demands pull in different directions, and no single release collapses them. Which is why a team that learns the split keeps the skill, and a team that learns "we use X here" has to relearn every time the landscape moves.

:::checklist The four questions that settle it | How long is the input — a paragraph or a bundle of documents? | How many times will I do this today — once, or thirty? | Does it have to sound like us, sustained, all the way through? | If this output were wrong and nobody caught it, what's the worst outcome?

That last question is the one that matters most, and it's the one people skip. If the honest answer involves a regulator, a patient, a court or a roof, the job is in the third category and the tool choice is irrelevant.

## Where the rule gets misapplied

**Treating "long" as "important".** A two-line email to your most valuable client is high-stakes and short. Stakes decide how carefully you check the output; length decides which tool you reach for. Two different axes.

**Assuming the fast side means careless.** Volume work still needs a check step. The difference is that the check is quick because the output is short, not because it doesn't matter.

**Using the third category as an excuse.** "Our work is regulated, so none of this applies" is the most expensive sentence in professional services. The regulated judgement stays with the qualified person. The tender, the induction pack, the standard explanatory letter, the monthly report and the policy document do not — and that's where the hours are. We take this apart on the [legal and conveyancing](/industries/legal-conveyancing/) and [allied health](/industries/allied-health/) tracks, where the boundary is sharpest and the misapplication is most common.

## What we don't do

We don't resell either tool. There's no partner arrangement, no affiliate link and no referral fee in either direction, and where the free tier is genuinely sufficient for the work we say so. That's not a virtue signal — it's the reason the recommendation is worth anything. A trainer who is paid on one vendor's seats is a sales channel with a whiteboard.

The other half of that is the account question. Which tier you're on changes what happens to the text you paste in, and that's a separate decision from which tool suits the job — we covered [why the same tool gives two different answers about your data](/insights/free-tier-paid-tier-your-data/) in its own article.

## The version to put on the wall

Long and voice-sensitive, one way. Fast and high-volume, the other. Regulated judgement, neither. Check everything that leaves the building.

Four lines, no version numbers, still true next year. That's the whole of [which tool for which job](/tools/which-tool/), and it's the part of the training people quote back to us months later.
